Sociologist

How social networks shape power, inequality, and expertise.

I study how connections between people, organizations, and institutions shape outcomes in markets, governance, and the environment—combining social network analysis, organizational theory, and economic sociology.

I am Professor of Sociology at the Department of Sociology, University of Copenhagen, and my main areas of research and teaching are economic sociology, political economy, social stratification, and social network analysis.

My work uses a distinctly relational approach: I trace how concrete social relations reproduce or transform structures of power, influence, and reward. Methodologically I work across quantitative and qualitative traditions, increasingly with large-scale register and network data. My research has appeared in journals including the American Journal of Sociology, Socio-Economic Review, Sociological Science, and general-science outlets such as Nature, Nature Human Behaviour, and PNAS.

Research

Four themes, one relational lens

My research asks how norms, behaviors, and resources spread through unequal structures of social and institutional relationships—what I think of as complex network contagion—and what that means for some of today's most pressing social problems.

01 — EMERGING FOCUS

The mental health crisis

My next major project, NeuroNet (funded by the Independent Research Fund Denmark), asks how children's chances of an ADHD or autism diagnosis are shaped by the networks of families, schools, and health professionals around them—and whether diagnoses propagate through those networks in ways that explain stark geographic variation.

02

Labor market inequality

Working with the Comparative Organizational Inequality Network, I study where rising labor market inequality comes from—between workplaces, within jobs, across cities—and how networks shape hiring, mobility, and who gets ahead.

03

Elites and democracy

I map the social organization of corporate and policy elites and how dominant ideas spread through government—from interlocking corporate boards to the economists who shape policy paradigms.

04

Climate and sustainability

From transnational sustainability standards to community forestry in Tanzania, I study how hybrid public–private governance networks form and whether they actually protect the environment and local livelihoods.

The gendered intergenerational transmission of managerial status

2025 Nature

Immigrant–native pay gap driven by lack of access to high-paying jobs

Hermansen, A., A. Penner, I. Boza, M. Elvira, O. Godechot, L. Henriksen et al.

Draws on data from nine countries to decompose the immigrant–native earnings gap. Finds roughly three-quarters of the gap comes from immigrants being sorted into lower-paying jobs, not unequal pay for the same work. Points policy attention toward access to good jobs rather than within-job discrimination.

What makes a contact valuable? Hiring, organizational networks and the advantages of network closure

Henriksen, L., T. Lyttelton and E. B. Bright

Examines which kinds of personal contacts actually help people get hired, using Danish administrative data on hiring. Studies community forestry in Southeast Tanzania to ask when certification schemes actually protect forests. Teaching

In the classroom

Teaching has always been central to my academic practice. I have developed and taught courses across the full curriculum—from introductory undergraduate teaching in economic sociology and network analysis to advanced PhD courses in organizational theory and research design.

Recurring courses include Introduction to Economic Sociology, Advanced Economic Sociology, Organizational Strategy in Network Perspective, and Perspectives in Organizational Analysis (PhD). I teach theory, methodology, and substantive topics alike, with a particular interest in bringing social network analysis, computational methods, and mixed-methods research to students at every level.
